This is MARSAD one, from Team Kanban: a three U CubeSat that watches the UAE's coastal waters for harmful algal blooms.
[pause 0.7]
Red tides are a real threat in the Gulf.
In 2008 and 2009, a bloom lasted for months, killed fish along the coast, and forced desalination plants to cut output or shut down.
Desalination supplies most of the UAE's drinking water, so operators need a dedicated, daily early warning.
[pause 0.7]
MARSAD one flies in a five hundred and fifty kilometre sun-synchronous orbit, crossing the UAE every day at the same local time.
Our success criterion: one usable, cloud-free image of UAE waters every day, and a bloom map to the ground team within twenty four hours, for six months.
The payload computes the Normalised Difference Chlorophyll Index on board, comparing red-edge and red light, so only the alerts need to come down.
[pause 0.7]
Here is the system.
An ESP thirty two S3 is the flight computer.
The payload pairs an A S seven three four one spectral sensor with a five megapixel camera.
An inertial sensor, GPS and three magnetorquers handle attitude.
A LoRa radio with a deployable tape antenna talks to our Abu Dhabi ground station.
Six solar cells charge a two-cell lithium-ion pack, and a Kapton heater keeps the battery warm in eclipse.
[pause 0.7]
Each ninety five minute orbit has four modes: forty minutes charging, ten minutes imaging, ten minutes over the ground station, and thirty five minutes in eclipse safe mode.
With everything on, the load is three point nine watts against six point six watts generated: a two point seven watt margin.
The satellite weighs zero point eight five kilograms of a four kilogram limit, and the parts cost one thousand four hundred and twenty dirhams, under our three thousand dirham budget.
[pause 0.7]
For the prototype, we started in Tinkercad.
An Arduino stands in for the flight computer, with a battery temperature sensor, a light sensor for eclipse, a knob for battery voltage, three mode lights and a servo for the antenna.
The firmware uses the same priority order as the flight software: power safety first, then the ground pass, then imaging.
Here it runs: imaging mode, the antenna deploying five seconds after boot, and a ground pass every ten seconds, while one JSON packet streams out every second.
[pause 0.7]
The flight firmware for the ESP thirty two S3 is written and reads the real sensors: power, battery and board temperature, and the spectral bloom index.
These shots are AI visualisations of that bench build; assembling and testing the physical board is our next step.
[pause 0.7]
Every packet feeds our mission control dashboard, built to the SpacePoint design manual: link status and packet age, colour-coded limits, time-stamped alarms, and a published data contract.
To test it, we inject faults.
A drained battery drops below twenty percent: the tiles turn red and the spacecraft enters safe mode.
A cold battery trips the temperature alarm.
When the link drops, the data goes stale after three seconds, and the link is declared lost after ten.
Clear the faults, and everything recovers on its own.
[pause 0.7]
So far, seven of our thirteen tests pass in simulation, from the telemetry stream to antenna deployment and ground-pass scheduling.
Four fault cases are verified through dashboard fault injection, and two need the physical board: the live USB link and the flight sensors.
